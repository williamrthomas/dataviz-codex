# Data Humanism School

## Overview

The Data Humanism school holds that data is always partial, contextual, and made by humans --
it is not objective, not complete, and not neutral. Visualization should embrace, rather than
suppress, imprecision, uncertainty, and personal context.

Where other schools optimize for clarity, efficiency, or perceptual accuracy, data humanism
insists that the body, emotions, and subjective experience belong in visualization. Handcraft,
texture, and individuality carry meaning that pixel-perfect digital tools cannot replicate.
This school emerged as a deliberate reaction to the dominance of minimalist and scientific
approaches, forcing the field to interrogate its assumptions about objectivity and audience.

## Key Figures

- [Giorgia Lupi](../thinkers/contemporary/giorgia-lupi.md) -- The school's most prominent
  advocate. Lupi's "Data Humanism" manifesto (2017) called for visualization that embraces
  complexity, imperfection, and human context rather than reducing everything to clean,
  efficient abstractions. Her work with Stefanie Posavec on *Dear Data* and her subsequent
  commercial practice at Pentagram have demonstrated that data humanism can operate at every
  scale, from intimate personal projects to major brand campaigns.

- [Stefanie Posavec](../thinkers/contemporary/stefanie-posavec.md) -- Co-creator of
  *Dear Data*, Posavec brings a design and typography background to data visualization. Her
  work emphasizes the materiality of data -- that the physical act of collecting, encoding,
  and drawing data by hand produces insights and intimacies that automated tools cannot.

- [W.E.B. Du Bois](../thinkers/historical/web-du-bois.md) -- Recognized as a spiritual
  ancestor of data humanism. Du Bois's hand-drawn data visualizations for the 1900 Paris
  Exposition used bold color, unconventional forms, and deliberate aesthetic choices to
  present data about Black life in America. His work demonstrated, over a century before
  the term was coined, that data visualization could be simultaneously rigorous, beautiful,
  and political.

## Core Tenets

- **Data is always partial, contextual, and made by humans:** Every dataset reflects choices
  about what to measure, how to measure it, who is counted, and who is not. Data humanism
  insists that these choices be acknowledged, not hidden behind a veneer of objectivity.

- **Visualization should embrace imprecision, uncertainty, and personal context:** Rather
  than suppressing the messiness of real-world data, the humanist approach makes it visible.
  Uncertainty is not a failure to be minimized but a truth to be communicated.

- **The body, emotions, and subjective experience belong in visualization:** Data humanism
  rejects the idea that visualization should be purely cognitive and analytical. Emotional
  responses, physical engagement, and personal connections to data are legitimate and
  valuable.

- **Handcraft, texture, and individuality carry meaning:** The act of drawing data by hand --
  choosing colors, feeling the weight of each data point, making marks that are imperfect
  and personal -- produces a different relationship to data than clicking buttons in
  software. The medium is part of the message.

- **Data represents people:** When data is about human beings, the visualization has an
  ethical obligation to honor the humanity behind the numbers. Aggregation and abstraction
  can erase the individual lives that data describes.

## Canonical Texts

- **Dear Data** (Lupi & Posavec, 2016) -- A year-long correspondence project in which Lupi
  and Posavec tracked personal data weekly and sent hand-drawn visualizations to each other
  on postcards. The book is both an artifact and a manifesto: it demonstrates that data
  visualization can be intimate, personal, imperfect, and profoundly meaningful.

- **Lupi's "Data Humanism" manifesto** (2017) -- Published on the Pentagram blog, this essay
  articulated the school's principles directly: data is not cold, not objective, and not
  complete. Visualization should honor complexity and humanity rather than reducing
  everything to clean lines and efficient encodings.

- **W.E.B. Du Bois's Data Portraits: Visualizing Black America** (Battle-Baptiste & Rusert,
  eds., 2018) -- A collection of Du Bois's Paris Exposition visualizations, recovered and
  contextualized. These hand-drawn charts are now recognized as foundational works in the
  data humanism tradition.

## Influence on the Field

Data humanism has shifted the conversation in visualization in several important ways:

- **Personal data visualization:** The *Dear Data* project inspired a wave of personal data
  projects -- individuals tracking and visualizing their own daily experiences, habits, and
  emotions. This genre barely existed before Lupi and Posavec popularized it.

- **Qualitative visualization:** Data humanism has opened space for visualizing qualitative,
  subjective, and narrative data -- types of information that the scientific and
  grammar-of-graphics schools typically exclude.

- **Data and empathy:** The school has influenced how practitioners think about the
  relationship between data and empathy. If data represents people, then visualization has
  an ethical obligation to honor the humanity behind the numbers.

- **Data physicalization:** The emerging movement to create physical, tangible data
  representations -- 3D-printed models, woven textiles, sculptural installations -- draws
  directly on data humanism's emphasis on embodiment and materiality.

- **Education:** Data humanism has been widely adopted in design schools and workshops as a
  way to teach data literacy. Drawing data by hand before using software is now a common
  pedagogical technique.

- **Du Bois's rediscovery:** The renewed interest in W.E.B. Du Bois's visualizations -- now
  exhibited in museums and taught in design programs worldwide -- is inseparable from the
  data humanism movement's emphasis on hand-drawn, politically engaged, aesthetically
  ambitious data work.

- **Questioning "objectivity":** Data humanism has made it acceptable -- even necessary -- to
  question the apparent objectivity of data and the visualizations that display it. This
  critical stance has influenced data ethics, data feminism, and critical data studies.

## Tensions with Other Schools

- **With the Minimalist/Tuftean School:** The most direct and productive conflict in
  contemporary visualization. Tufte sees chartjunk as dishonesty; Lupi sees it as humanity.
  Tufte argues for maximum data-ink ratio; Lupi argues that non-data elements (texture,
  handcraft, decoration) carry meaning. Both are right -- for different goals and audiences.
  The tension forces each school to articulate what it actually values. See
  [Minimalist / Tuftean](minimalist-tuftean.md).

- **With the Scientific/Perceptual School:** The scientific school questions whether imprecise
  or decorative visualization serves readers well. If the goal is accurate perception of
  quantities, then handcraft and embellishment are noise. Data humanism responds that
  accurate perception of quantities is not the only valid goal -- that emotional engagement,
  personal connection, and critical awareness of data's limitations are equally important.
  See [Scientific / Perceptual](scientific-perceptual.md).

- **With the Narrative/Storytelling School:** The narrative school assumes a clear,
  communicable message. Data humanism questions whether data always yields a clear story, and
  whether the impulse to narrate can flatten the complexity and ambiguity that data carries.
  See [Narrative / Storytelling](narrative-storytelling.md).

- **These tensions are productive:** Data humanism forces the field to interrogate its
  assumptions about objectivity, audience, and purpose. The field is richer for having a
  school that insists data is human, not just numbers.

## Representative Examples

- **Dear Data postcards** (Lupi & Posavec) -- Hand-drawn weekly data visualizations
  exchanged by mail, covering everything from door usage to complaints to laughter. Each
  postcard is unique, personal, and imperfect -- and profoundly informative about the
  human experience of data.

- **W.E.B. Du Bois's Paris Exposition charts** (1900) -- Hand-drawn visualizations of Black
  economic and social life in America, using bold color, spiral forms, and unconventional
  layouts. Created over a century before data humanism was named, they embody its every
  principle.

- **Lupi's "Data Items" series** -- Personal data turned into wearable art, bridging data
  visualization and physical design. Demonstrates that data can live outside the screen.

- **Data physicalization installations** -- Sculptures, textiles, and 3D objects that encode
  data in physical form, making data tangible and embodied. Projects by Domestic Data
  Streamers and others exemplify this approach.

- **Mimi Onuoha's "The Library of Missing Datasets"** -- An art installation that visualizes
  data that does not exist -- highlighting the human choices about what gets measured and
  what gets ignored. A quintessential data humanist work that makes absence visible.
