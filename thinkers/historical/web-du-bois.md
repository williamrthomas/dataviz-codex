# W.E.B. Du Bois (1868--1963)
> Du Bois used data visualization to counter racist narratives about African Americans, demonstrating through meticulous statistics that progress, complexity, and humanity were present in Black life.

## Overview

W.E.B. Du Bois -- sociologist, historian, civil rights activist, and one of the most important American intellectuals of the 20th century -- is now recognized as a foundational figure in data visualization. For the 1900 Paris Exposition Universelle, Du Bois and his students at Atlanta University created a portfolio of over 60 hand-drawn charts, graphs, and maps collectively known as "data portraits," which documented the social and economic conditions of African Americans in Georgia. These visualizations were simultaneously rigorous statistical evidence and political art: they were designed to counter racist narratives by demonstrating, through meticulous data, the progress, complexity, and humanity of Black life in America.

Du Bois's visualizations were striking in their modernity. Created decades before the formalization of graphic design principles, they featured bold colors, unusual chart forms, spiral and fan-shaped diagrams, and visual experimentation far ahead of their time. His work was largely forgotten for most of the 20th century until a 2018 publication -- *W.E.B. Du Bois's Data Portraits: Visualizing Black America* -- sparked a major reassessment of his visual legacy. The Cooper Hewitt (Smithsonian Design Museum) staged a major 2023 exhibition on his visualizations, cementing his recognition as one of the field's most original and important practitioners.

## Key Contributions

- **Created 60+ hand-drawn "data portraits" for the 1900 Paris Exposition Universelle** -- a portfolio of charts, graphs, and maps documenting African American life in Georgia
- **Pioneered infographics as a tool of social justice and political argument** -- using data visualization explicitly to counter racist narratives
- **Demonstrated visual experimentation decades ahead of his time** -- bold colors, unusual chart forms, spiral diagrams, and fan-shaped layouts that anticipate modern data art
- **Combined rigorous statistical evidence with political art** -- his data was simultaneously analytical and persuasive
- **Pioneered the use of sociological data for advocacy** -- his *Philadelphia Negro* was one of the first rigorous sociological studies using statistical evidence

## Major Works

- *The Georgia Negro: A Social Study* -- the portfolio of 60+ charts presented at the 1900 Paris Exposition (see [Du Bois Data Portraits case study](../../case-studies/du-bois-data-portraits.md))
- *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) -- pioneering sociological study using statistical evidence to document the conditions of Black life in Philadelphia
- *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) -- while primarily a literary and sociological work, it reflects Du Bois's commitment to using evidence to challenge racial assumptions
- *W.E.B. Du Bois's Data Portraits: Visualizing Black America* (2018, eds. Whitney Battle-Baptiste and Britt Rusert) -- the modern publication that brought his visual work back to public attention

## Core Philosophy

Du Bois used data visualization to counter racist narratives about African Americans, demonstrating through meticulous statistics that progress, complexity, and humanity were present in Black life. His data was simultaneously rigorous evidence and political art. The visualizations were striking in their modernity: bold colors, unusual chart forms, and visual experimentation far ahead of their time.

His philosophy encompassed several intertwined commitments:

1. **Data as counter-narrative**: In an era of pervasive scientific racism, Du Bois recognized that carefully collected and clearly presented data could directly challenge false claims about racial inferiority. The data portraits were designed to make denial of Black achievement and progress intellectually impossible.
2. **Aesthetics as argument**: Du Bois understood that the *form* of a visualization carries persuasive weight. His bold, unconventional designs were not decorative choices -- they were rhetorical strategies designed to command attention and convey dignity.
3. **Visualization for human dignity**: Unlike visualizations designed for purely analytical purposes, Du Bois's charts were made to affirm the humanity and complexity of the people they represented. Each chart was an act of respect toward its subjects.

Du Bois was working in the tradition of [William Playfair](./william-playfair.md) and [Florence Nightingale](./florence-nightingale.md) (using data to persuade) but extending it explicitly toward questions of racial justice and human dignity.

## Connections

- Working in the tradition of [William Playfair](./william-playfair.md) (data for communication) and [Florence Nightingale](./florence-nightingale.md) (data for advocacy), but extending it explicitly toward questions of **racial justice and human dignity**

- Du Bois is a **key ancestor of the [data humanism](../../schools/data-humanism.md) school** -- the contemporary movement that insists data visualization should center human experience and challenge power structures
- His visual experimentation anticipates the work of modern data artists like **Giorgia Lupi** (who coined "data humanism") and **Nadieh Bremer**

- His work connects to the broader tradition of visualization for social justice, which includes [Florence Nightingale's](./florence-nightingale.md) public health advocacy and [John Snow's](./john-snow.md) cholera investigation

## Influence

Du Bois is now recognized as a foundational figure in the tradition of data visualization for social justice. His work anticipates modern data journalism and advocacy visualization. The Cooper Hewitt (Smithsonian Design Museum) staged a major 2023 exhibition on his visualizations, cementing his recognition.

His lasting influence operates on several levels:

1. **Data visualization for social justice**: Du Bois established that visualization is not a neutral analytical tool but a medium that can be wielded for political and moral purposes. This tradition runs through contemporary data journalism, advocacy organizations, and activist data projects.
2. **Visual experimentation**: His unconventional chart forms -- spirals, fans, and bold geometric compositions -- demonstrated that the vocabulary of data visualization extends far beyond standard bars and lines. This spirit of formal experimentation is alive in modern data art and creative visualization.
3. **The recovery of hidden histories**: The rediscovery of Du Bois's visualization work is itself a lesson in how the contributions of marginalized practitioners are erased and must be actively recovered. His example has prompted broader reassessments of whose contributions are included in the field's canon.
4. **Data humanism**: Du Bois's insistence that data represents human lives -- and that visualizations should honor that -- directly prefigures the contemporary [data humanism](../../schools/data-humanism.md) movement.

## Key Quotes

- Du Bois's charts were designed to show "the progress, complexity, and humanity present in Black life" -- using data as a tool of dignity and counter-narrative
- The visual rhetoric of the data portraits conveyed a message beyond their statistical content: that Black Americans were not objects of study but subjects with agency, achievement, and complexity
- "The problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color line" -- while from *The Souls of Black Folk*, this conviction animated all of Du Bois's data work
