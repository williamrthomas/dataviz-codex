# Fernanda Viegas & Martin Wattenberg (active 2000s--present)
> "Visualization is most powerful when it enables discovery -- revealing patterns in data that surprise even the people who made the visualization."

## Overview

Fernanda Viegas and Martin Wattenberg are a collaborative duo who have shaped data visualization at the intersection of research, art, and public engagement. Working together since the early 2000s, they co-created Many Eyes -- the pioneering collaborative web-based visualization platform at IBM Research -- and the famous Wind Map, a real-time visualization of US wind patterns that entered the permanent collection of the Museum of Modern Art. They currently co-lead Google's PAIR (People+AI Research) initiative, where they work on making artificial intelligence transparent and interpretable.

Their work is distinguished by a commitment to the social and collaborative dimensions of data visualization. Where most visualization tools are designed for individual analysts, Viegas and Wattenberg have consistently asked: what happens when visualization becomes a shared, community-forming activity? This question drove Many Eyes, influenced the design of their art installations, and shapes their current work on AI interpretability.

## Key Contributions

- **Co-created Many Eyes** (IBM Research, 2007) -- the first large-scale public collaborative visualization platform, demonstrating that online collaborative visualization was possible years before it became mainstream
- **Created the Wind Map** (2012) -- a real-time visualization of US wind patterns that became an iconic work of data art and entered MoMA's permanent collection
- **Co-lead Google PAIR** -- pioneering research on AI transparency and interpretability through visualization
- **Bridged visualization, art, and research** -- demonstrating that the same work can advance scientific understanding, serve the public, and function as art
- **Pioneered the social dimension of data visualization** -- showing that visualization can be collaborative and community-forming, not just individual or expert-facing

## Core Philosophy

Viegas and Wattenberg believe that visualization is most powerful when it enables *discovery* -- revealing patterns in data that surprise even the people who made the visualization. This emphasis on surprise and discovery sets their work apart from visualization approaches focused primarily on communication or confirmation.

They also believe deeply in the social dimension of data: visualization can be collaborative and community-forming, not just individual or expert-facing. Many Eyes was built on the insight that people want to share and discuss visualizations, not just consume them in isolation. This social vision anticipated the data journalism and social media visualization movements by several years.

Their current work at Google PAIR extends this philosophy to artificial intelligence: if AI systems are to be trusted, their internal workings must be made visible and interpretable. Visualization, they argue, is essential infrastructure for responsible AI.

## Connections

- Wattenberg co-designed **ordered treemaps** with [Ben Shneiderman](../modern/ben-shneiderman.md), winning the IEEE Test of Time Award (2021)
- Their Wind Map influenced how weather data is visualized across major media organizations, connecting to the [journalistic school](../../schools/journalistic.md)

- Their art installations connect to the [artistic-expressive school](../../schools/artistic-expressive.md) alongside [Giorgia Lupi](./giorgia-lupi.md) and [Stefanie Posavec](./stefanie-posavec.md)

- Their collaborative platform work anticipated tools built on [Jeffrey Heer](./jeffrey-heer.md)'s Vega ecosystem

## Influence

Many Eyes demonstrated that online collaborative visualization was possible years before it became mainstream, anticipating an entire generation of web-based visualization tools and platforms. The platform showed that non-experts could create meaningful visualizations when given the right tools -- a democratic vision that influenced everything from Tableau Public to Observable.

Their Wind Map became one of the most iconic data visualizations of the 2010s, influencing how weather data is visualized across major media companies and demonstrating that real-time data streaming could produce work of genuine beauty. Its inclusion in MoMA's collection helped legitimize data visualization as an art form.

Their PAIR work at Google is shaping how AI systems are made transparent and interpretable to non-specialists, extending the reach of visualization into one of the most consequential technology domains of the 21st century.
