# Michael Friendly (b. ~1944)
> "Understanding how visualization evolved is essential to understanding how it should be practiced."

## Overview

Michael Friendly is the historian, statistician, and methodologist who gave the field of data visualization a proper intellectual genealogy. Working at York University from the 1980s to the present, Friendly has been the foremost chronicler of the field's history, creating the "Milestones" timeline that traces the evolution of data visualization from its earliest origins to the present day. He is also a significant technical contributor, having developed mosaic plots and other graphical methods for categorical data.

Friendly's work ensures that data visualization is understood not as a recent invention of the digital age but as a tradition with deep roots stretching back centuries through statistics, cartography, and science. His Milestones Project (datavis.ca/milestones) is the most comprehensive timeline of visualization history available, and his collaboration with Howard Wainer produced the definitive scholarly history of the field. Beyond history, Friendly's HistData R package makes the datasets behind iconic historical visualizations -- Snow's cholera map, Nightingale's rose diagram, Minard's Napoleon graphic -- reproducible and accessible to modern analysts.

## Key Contributions

- **Milestones Project:** The most comprehensive timeline of data visualization history, tracing the field from its origins through the present (datavis.ca/milestones)
- **Co-author of the definitive history of data visualization:** Partnered with Howard Wainer on *A History of Data Visualization and Graphic Communication* (2021)
- **Mosaic plots:** Developed graphical methods for visualizing cross-tabulated categorical data
- **HistData R package:** Makes classic historical datasets (Snow, Nightingale, Minard, etc.) available for modern statistical computing, enabling researchers and students to reproduce iconic visualizations
- **Visualizing Categorical Data:** Authored a comprehensive treatment of graphical methods for categorical and multivariate data

## Core Philosophy

Friendly believes that the history of data visualization is not an academic luxury but a practical necessity. Understanding how the field evolved -- what worked, what failed, what was forgotten and rediscovered -- is essential to improving how visualization is practiced today. He sees historical scholarship as a form of quality assurance: by studying the masters (Playfair, Nightingale, Minard, Snow), modern practitioners can learn principles that transcend any particular technology or software tool.

His technical work on categorical data visualization reflects a complementary belief: that many common data types (contingency tables, cross-tabulations, multi-way categorical data) are poorly served by standard chart types, and that dedicated graphical methods can reveal structure and relationships that would otherwise remain hidden.

## Influence

Friendly's historical scholarship has done more than anyone's to establish a proper intellectual history for the field of data visualization. Before Friendly's systematic work, the field's history was fragmentary -- scattered across statistics, cartography, graphic design, and computer science. Friendly wove these threads into a coherent narrative that demonstrates the continuity and cumulative progress of visualization as a discipline.

His HistData R package has had an outsized practical impact by making the data behind iconic historical visualizations reproducible for modern analysts. When a student today recreates Snow's cholera map, Nightingale's rose diagram, or Minard's graphic of Napoleon's march in R or Python, they are working with data that Friendly curated and made accessible. This bridges centuries of visualization practice, connecting contemporary data scientists to the tradition's founding achievements.

The Milestones Project serves as the field's definitive reference timeline and is cited by researchers, educators, and practitioners worldwide. It is the resource that visualization courses, textbooks, and histories consult when they need to place developments in chronological and intellectual context.
